Phase 1Thesis model on SEC filings

The story in one minute

Quality is excellent. Margins hit a record, services keep compounding, and cash returns are huge. The risks are China, App Store rules and a slower iPhone cycle.

Apple is an excellent business growing at an ordinary pace. The scorecard shows this at a glance (). Ranked against every company in the S&P 500, Apple scores 9.4 out of 10 for profitability, 9.0 for shareholder returns and 8.1 for financial health. Growth is the weakest of the four at 5.2, close to the middle of the pack. Sales reached $416B in fiscal 2025, 6.4% more than the year before and 78% more than in 2015. Profit grew faster: $112B against $53B in 2015, more than twice as much. The fifth score, value, moves with the price typed in the box at the top.

Services led the year’s good news. The card next to the scorecard, “What changed since the last report”, leads with it. Services sales passed $109B, up 13.5%, about twice the pace of total sales. Services are now 26% of sales but more than 40% of gross profit. They keep about 75 cents of every dollar as gross profit, against about 37 cents for hardware. Helped by that mix, gross margin reached a record 46.9%, 0.7 points above last year. But the same card shows the other side. Greater China sales fell 4% to about $64B, the third weak year in four, and the risk factors now say more about US tariffs.

That sets up the question the rest of the report keeps asking. Earnings per share rose from $6.08 to $7.47, a gain of $1.39. Nearly half of it, $0.66, came from a one-time 2024 tax charge that did not repeat. The lower share count added another $0.21: Apple paid out $106B in buybacks and dividends, and the share count fell 2.7%. Meanwhile iPhone, still half of sales, grew only about 2% a year over four years, against about 12% a year for services. So the big question is simple. Can services and buybacks keep earnings per share growing while iPhone barely grows and China shrinks?

How Apple makes money

iPhone is half of sales, services is the growth engine. Services grew from 19% to 26% of sales in four years, and it carries about twice the gross margin of hardware (75% against 37%).

The first thing to know is that iPhone is still half the business. In fiscal 2025 it brought in $209.6B of Apple’s $416B in sales, or 50.4% (). That was 4.2% more than the year before, a solid year for a product this big. But its slice is slowly thinning: it was 52.5% in fiscal 2021. The other devices matter less each year. Mac, iPad and Wearables and home together brought in $97.4B, about 23% of sales. Wearables and home has shrunk three years in a row, from $41.2B in fiscal 2022 to $35.7B.

Second, services did almost all of the growing. Services is the money Apple earns from things like the App Store rather than from selling devices. It rose from $68.4B in fiscal 2021 to $109.2B, up 60% in four years, or about 12% a year (). Over the same years total sales grew by $50.3B, and services supplied $40.8B of that, about four dollars in every five. All the hardware lines together grew just 3% in four years. That is how services went from 19% to 26% of sales.

But sales understate how much services matters. Each dollar of services leaves about 75 cents of gross profit, the money left after the direct cost of providing it. Each dollar of hardware leaves about 37 cents (). The gap has widened: the services margin rose 5.7 points since fiscal 2021, products only 1.5 points. Put together, services made about $82B of gross profit in fiscal 2025, against about $113B from all devices. So a quarter of sales now brings in more than 40% of gross profit, up from about 31% four years earlier. That mix shift helped lift the company’s gross margin to a record 46.9%.

Where the money comes from, and where things are made

Sold everywhere, made mostly in Asia. The Americas give 43% of sales. China is 15% of sales and a major assembly base, so trade rules and tariffs hit twice.

Sales are spread wide, and most of the growth is outside China. The Americas brought in $178.4B in fiscal 2025, 43% of the $416.2B total, and grew 3.9% a year over four years (). Europe is the quiet winner. Its sales rose from $89.3B to $111.0B, or 5.6% a year, and its slice of the total grew from 24% to 27%. Rest of Asia Pacific grew fastest, 6.3% a year, though it is still small, at $33.7B. Japan barely moved over four years, but its latest year was strong, up 14% to $28.7B.

Greater China is the exception, and it matters twice. Its sales peaked at $74.2B in fiscal 2022 and have fallen every year since, to $64.4B, 13% below the peak (). Its share of the total slipped from 19% to 15%. Without China, the rest of the company grew 4.3% a year over four years, against 3.3% with it. But this understates the exposure. China is also a major assembly base. The 10-K names China mainland, India and Vietnam among the places where outsourcing partners build “substantially all” of the hardware. Tariffs on goods made there can raise costs; weak demand there can cut sales.

The economies point the other way. Weighting each region’s growth outlook by its share of sales gives 2.2% a year (). China’s economy is forecast to grow 4.2%, more than double the US rate of 2.0%, yet Greater China is the only region where sales shrank. Europe’s currencies rose 6% against the dollar, which helps sales when they are counted in dollars. For tax, Ireland matters most. It has the most legal entities, 4 of the 13 listed (). The EU state-aid ruling there cost a one-time $10.2B in fiscal 2024. That charge dropping out added $0.66 to fiscal 2025 earnings per share, about half the $1.39 rise.

Phase 2Phase 4Its own filings, and other companies' filings that name Apple

Customers, suppliers and rivals

No customer is above 10% of sales, but many suppliers live on Apple. Two chip makers get most of their revenue from Apple, and a third close to half. When their numbers move, it often says something about iPhone orders first.

The first thing to see is who leans on whom. Apple names no customer that buys more than 10% of its sales, so its $416.2B of fiscal 2025 sales are spread across many buyers. Its suppliers look the other way round (). Cirrus Logic gets 89% of its revenue from Apple, almost nine dollars in every ten. Skyworks gets 63% and Qorvo 47%. Broadcom and Qualcomm each get 20% from Apple, one dollar in five. Across these five sample suppliers the simple average is 47.8%, so the typical one relies on Apple for close to half its business.

Second, that dependence makes suppliers an early signal. The iPhone brought in $209.6B in fiscal 2025, about half of Apple’s sales. When chip makers such as Cirrus Logic or Skyworks report, their numbers often say something about iPhone orders before Apple does. The dependence also runs the other way. Two companies that build for Apple, Foxconn and TSMC, sit on thin lines in only because no share is disclosed, not because the tie is weak. The 10-K says “substantially all” hardware is made by outsourcing partners.

Then there are the companies that sell for Apple. About 62% of net sales goes through others: carriers such as Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile, retailers such as Best Buy, and resellers (). Apple’s own stores and website take the other 38%. In dollars that is roughly $258B sold indirectly against $158B sold directly. No single carrier passes 10% of sales, yet carriers hold a large share of the money owed to Apple, so their financial health matters. On the dashed lines sit the rivals: Samsung, Alphabet and Xiaomi.

Owners and people

Owned mostly by index funds; insiders only sell. The three biggest holders are passive managers. Insider sales are planned sales of vested stock; nobody bought on the open market in 12 months.

The biggest group of Apple owners is funds that do not trade on a view. Index and passive funds hold 31% of the shares, almost twice the 17% held by active funds that pick stocks (). Add other institutions and professional money owns 63%, which leaves 37% with individuals and everyone else. Vanguard, BlackRock and State Street together own 21.1% of the company (). Index funds hold Apple because it is a large part of the indexes they track, not because of a view on the next iPhone. That tends to make the owner base steady: such funds sell mostly when savers pull money out.

The biggest cuts this quarter came from active managers. The three index giants each moved by 0.2 points or less, and two of them added. The biggest cut in the top eight came from Berkshire Hathaway, a stock picker rather than an index fund, down 0.3 points to 1.6%. Fidelity was down 0.2 points to 2.0%. But the quarterly changes understate a quieter shift. Apple's share count fell 2.7% last year, so a holder that keeps every share still ends up with a bigger slice. For Vanguard, that alone would lift 9.5% to about 9.8% in a year without buying a single share.

Insiders sold, but on autopilot. Executives and directors sold $92M of stock in 12 months, in 11 sales, all under 10b5-1 plans. These are schedules set in advance, so the timing of each sale is not picked trade by trade (). Nobody bought on the open market. October alone was $41M, 45% of the total. Set against the company, the sums are small: Apple spent $90.7B on buybacks in fiscal 2025, nearly 1,000 times as much. Oversight looks conventional too. Seven of eight directors are independent, and Tim Cook’s $74.6M of pay in fiscal 2024 was less than 0.1% of that year’s $93.7B profit.

Eleven years of the numbers

Sales up 78% since 2015, profit more than doubled. Margins widened as services grew, and the share count fell by 35%, so each share now owns much more of the profit.

First, profit grew faster than sales. Sales rose from $233.7B in fiscal 2015 to $416.2B in fiscal 2025, a gain of 78%. Net profit went from $53.4B to $112.0B, so it more than doubled (). The road was not smooth. Sales fell in 2016, 2019 and 2023, and gross margin sank to a low of 37.8% in 2019. Since then it has risen every year to a record 46.9%, as services, which now carry margins near 75%, became a bigger part of sales (). Out of each dollar of sales, Apple now keeps 26.9 cents as profit, against 22.8 cents in 2015.

Second, each share now owns a bigger slice. Buybacks cut the share count from 23.17 billion in 2015 to 15.00 billion, a fall of 35% (). So while profit doubled, earnings per share more than tripled, from $2.30 to $7.47. But last year’s rise, from $6.08 to $7.47, flatters the business. Almost half of the $1.39 gain, $0.66, came from a one-time $10.2B EU tax charge in fiscal 2024 dropping out (). Higher sales and a wider margin added $0.52 together, and fewer shares $0.21. Without that charge, the rise is 73 cents, about 12%, not 23%.

Third, the profit is backed by cash, and nearly all of it goes back to owners. Over eleven years, free cash flow, the cash left after running and investing in the business, added up to $882B, against $817B of reported profit (). Fiscal 2025 was softer: free cash flow fell 9% to $98.8B. Over the last five years buybacks and dividends came to $513B, about the same as the $511.5B of free cash flow (). Last year’s $106B payout topped free cash flow. Net cash, cash minus debt, fell from $66B in 2021 to $34B (). That is by design, but it cannot run forever.

Is the price right?

The first test is what the price has to believe. At the $191 example price, Apple is valued at about $2.87 trillion, or 25.6 times fiscal 2025 earnings per share of $7.47 (). Put another way, the business made $98.8B of free cash flow, so each dollar paid for the shares buys about 3.4 cents of cash a year. To justify $191, that cash has to grow about 5.8% a year for ten years. Over the last five years it grew about 6% a year, from $73.4B in 2020. The price asks the past to repeat, not to improve.

Second, all five methods bracket the $191 example price. This report’s cash-flow model puts fair value between $183 and $230, with a base case of $204, about 7% above $191 (). The spread comes from the discount rate, the yearly return an owner demands: half a point higher cuts the value by $21, half a point lower adds $26. The bear-to-bull range is wider, $115 to $272, because it also changes growth. The three phase 3 rows give $164 to $261 on 22 to 35 times earnings, $150 to $246 on peers and $169 to $260 over 52 weeks. $191 sits inside every bar.

But this leans on a higher multiple than Apple used to get. From 2015 to 2019 the shares averaged about 15.5 times earnings in this sample history. Since 2020 the yearly average has not dropped below 25 times, and it reached 34.0 times in 2025 (). At 25.6 times, the $191 example price sits near the bottom of that newer band. The peer chart shows the trade-off (). Apple’s 32.0% operating margin matches Alphabet’s 32.1% and is nearly five times Dell’s 6.6%. Yet its sales grew 8.7% a year, about half Alphabet’s 17.7% and below Microsoft, Meta and Amazon. It outgrows Cisco, Dell and HP but trails the other four peers.

A breakout that held: buyers took back the $186 ceiling

The month ended 5.4% higher, and above a price that had turned buyers away three times. For a long-term owner this says little about value. It shows where buyers and sellers now meet, and which prices the market is likely to test next.

The month began inside a box. From 18 September to 2 October the shares moved between $177 and $186, and every run at the top of that box failed (marks A on ). Three times buyers pushed to within 20 cents of $186, and three times the shares closed below it. The bottom held just as firmly. On 25 September the price dipped to $177.0, then closed at $181.5, near the top of the day’s range (mark B). Price action traders read a day like that as sellers running out of shares to sell.

The fourth attempt worked. On 5 October one wide up-day carried the price from $185.5 to $189.0 (mark C). Its body was twice the size of a normal day before it, and 88 million shares changed hands, about 1.6 times the usual volume (). The price action book calls a candle like this a breakout: a big bar that closes beyond a level the market has been watching. The bigger the bar and the more important the level, the more likely the move carries on.

What came next mattered more. The breakout was tested. By 9 October the shares had slipped back to $186.3, only 30 cents above the old ceiling, and buyers stepped in again (mark D). Old resistance had become support, the textbook sign that a breakout is real. In the last 15 trading days not one close fell below the 20-day average, which is the book’s test for a strong trend ().

The move is not stretched, but it is close to a target. Adding the height of the old box ($9) to the breakout level points to about $195. The shares reached $193.8 on 15 October, then closed lower that day and the next (mark E), a first sign of profit taking near the target. Five days in a row of higher lows is still far from the ten-day streak the book treats as a climax.

What could happen next

Base case: sales grow about 6% a year to $557B by 2030. Three scenarios, each with its assumptions in plain view. These are our model's numbers, not analyst consensus.

The base case asks for nothing new. It has sales growing 6% a year, from $416B in fiscal 2025 to $557B in 2030, the middle dashed line on . That is close to the pace of the past decade. Sales rose from $234B in 2015 to $416B, about 5.9% a year, and fiscal 2025 alone grew 6.4%. The shaded fan shows how far outcomes can spread. By 2030 the bear line ends near $460B and the bull line near $640B. That gap of about $181B equals about 43% of fiscal 2025 sales, so differences in yearly growth add up fast.

Value per share depends on more than growth. pairs each growth path with a margin and a buyback pace. The base case keeps the operating margin at 32%, unchanged from fiscal 2025, and shrinks the share count 3% a year, close to last year’s 2.7%. Its value is $204 a share, 7% above the $191 example price. The bear case needs iPhone sales to shrink and services growth to slow to 6%, against 13.5% now. Its margin falls to 29% and its value to $115, 40% below the example price. The bull case needs AI features to start a new upgrade cycle, and its value is $272, 42% above.

Another big swing comes from an input Apple does not control. holds base growth and turns two dials. One is the discount rate, the yearly return an investor asks for. The other is long-run growth, the pace assumed after the forecast years. Raise the discount rate by half a point, from 7.5% to 8.0%, and the base value drops from $204 to $183, below the example price. At 7.0% it rises to $230. Management’s guidance for next quarter, sales up 10% to 12% and gross margin of 47% to 48% against fiscal 2025’s record 46.9%, runs ahead of the base pace. But it covers three months, not five years.

What could break the thesis

Debt is easy to carry; concentration is the real risk. Half of sales is one product and 15% is one country that is also a major factory location.

The base story is solid, and the balance sheet is not where it would break. Apple owes about $99B against $132B of cash and securities, so it still holds about $34B more cash than debt. Repayments are spread out (). The heaviest year, 2028, brings $12.3B due, about an eighth of last year’s $98.8B of free cash flow. Everything due from 2026 to 2030 adds up to $45.3B, about a third of the cash pile, and more than half of the scheduled debt is not due until after 2030. Net cash has roughly halved since 2021, from about $66B, on purpose: the company aims for net cash near zero.

The first real risk is one product. iPhone brought in $209.6B of last year’s $416.2B, just over half of all sales. In 2021 that share was about 52%, so it has slipped only slowly. iPhone also sits on the lower-margin side of the business: hardware keeps about 37 cents of gross profit per dollar of sales, against 75 cents for services. Second, Greater China is both a market and a major factory location. It gave $64.4B, about 15% of sales, down 13% from $74.2B in 2022 while the other regions grew. A slump there would hit sales, and a trade fight could also raise costs.

Third, the rules are changing, and the filing says so. This year’s risk section adds new wording on US tariffs for products made in Asia. It speaks more strongly about App Store rules in the EU and US, and about AI competition. The wording on supply concentration is unchanged. But none of the kill switches has tripped yet. Services growth of 13.5% is well clear of its 8% line, and the 46.9% gross margin sits 2.9 points above 44%. The tightest switch is share count: shares fell 2.7%, only 0.7 points better than the 2% line. China, down 4%, is the one on watch.

What happened, in order

A quiet year in filings. Four results, one annual meeting vote, one bond sale. No acquisitions above the reporting line. Sample timeline.

The year opened with the filing this report reads. On 30 October 2025 Apple filed its fiscal 2025 results under 8-K item 2.02, the code for earnings releases. Sales came in at $416B, up 6.4% on the $391B of fiscal 2024. Gross margin reached 46.9%, a record and 0.7 points above the year before. Three months later, on 29 January 2026, the first-quarter results reported a record December quarter. So the year started from a high base, and every later filing would be judged against it.

Late winter and spring were about owners and money. On 24 February shareholders re-elected all eight directors, seven of them independent, and rejected the shareholder proposals (item 5.07, the code for vote results). On 1 May the second-quarter results came with a larger buyback programme. That builds on a habit: in fiscal 2025 Apple spent $90.7B on buybacks and $15.4B on dividends, about $106B in all, and the share count fell 2.7% in the year. Four days later came a bond sale to refinance notes coming due. The debt schedule shows $11.0B maturing in fiscal 2026.

Borrowing while sitting on cash looks odd, but it fits the plan. Apple ended fiscal 2025 with $132.4B of cash and securities against $98.7B of debt, so it could have paid those notes from cash. It chose to refinance them instead. That matches its stated aim of bringing net cash, $33.7B at the end of fiscal 2025, close to zero over time. The last filing on the timeline, on 30 July, showed services up double digits and iPhone flat. Services also led in fiscal 2025, growing 13.5% against 4.2% for iPhone. No deal was big enough to need its own filing.

The bottom line

Yes, for now: services and buybacks are carrying earnings per share while iPhone barely grows and China shrinks. The catch is that the cash pile paying for the buybacks is shrinking. The price also already assumes about 6% a year growth in free cash flow for ten years.

The first answer is yes, and fiscal 2025 shows how. Earnings per share rose from $6.08 to $7.47, a 23% gain. But that overstates the engine. Almost half of the rise, 66 cents, came from a one-time 2024 tax charge dropping out. Strip it away and the gain was 73 cents, about 12%. Services and buybacks carried most of it. Services sales grew 13.5% to $109B and earn more than 40% of gross profit on 26% of sales. Fewer shares added 21 cents. Meanwhile iPhone grew about 4%, Greater China fell 4%, and this year’s third-quarter results repeated the pattern: services up double digits, iPhone flat.

Second, the cost of keeping it going is rising. Apple paid owners $106B last year, $7B more than the $99B of free cash flow it made, and that cash flow fell 9%. The gap came out of savings: net cash dropped from $50B to $34B. Apple aims for net cash near zero, so this is by design, but in time buybacks will have to come from each year’s cash alone. Hardware also matters more than the services story suggests. iPhone is still half of sales, several chip suppliers live on Apple’s orders, and China is a 15% market and a major factory location, now facing new tariff wording in the risk factors.

The price already gives credit for all this. At the $191 example price the shares trade at about 26 times earnings, and that price sits inside this report’s model range of $183 to $230. It assumes free cash flow grows about 6% a year for ten years, the same pace as the last five. The base case has sales growing about 6% a year to $557B by 2030. The shares also rose 5.4% in the month, though the price chapter notes that says little about value. If the story fails, it should show first in the four tests in .

What would prove this story wrong

Each one is also a kill switch you can turn on
1Services growth slips below 8%. If that lasts two quarters, the engine that earns more than 40% of gross profit is fading. Last year it grew 13.5%, from $96B to $109B.
2Gross margin falls below 44%. It is 46.9%, a record. A drop of about 3 points would suggest that tariffs or App Store rules are biting.
3Greater China falls more than 10% in a year. Sales fell 4% to about $64B, the third weak year in four. China is also a major assembly base, so trade rules there hit Apple twice.
4The share count falls less than 2% in a year. It fell 2.7% last year and 35% since 2015. With net cash down to $34B, a slower pace would weaken one of the two supports for earnings per share.

The upshotthe answer stays yes while services grow faster than 8% and the share count falls by at least 2% a year. Today those read 13.5% and 2.7%. The first check is management’s guide for next quarter: sales growth of 10% to 12% and a gross margin of 47% to 48%.
